# How to review article capacity before upgrading a news website plan

A publishing allowance is useful only when it is compared with the work a newsroom can actually produce. An independent reporter may need room for a busy week without needing the largest available plan. Another publisher may regularly approach its limit while maintaining a consistent editorial process. The decision should come from a record of real work. This guide explains a monthly review that separates technical capacity, reporting effort and the value delivered to readers.

## Build a simple activity record

Start by listing the articles published during the previous month. Add a short label for the type of work involved: original reporting, a service update, an interview or an explanatory piece. This is an internal planning exercise, so it does not require a complicated scoring system. The labels help explain why two months with the same article count might have required very different amounts of effort.

A month containing several interviews and travel assignments can be demanding even if the final count is modest. Another month may contain more brief updates from a regular local event. Looking only at the number hides that difference. Record unfinished reporting as well, because it represents work already invested even when it has not produced a public page. Avoid treating unpublished investigation as wasted effort.

## Calculate a normal month

Use your typical week to estimate a month, then compare the estimate with the actual record. Four articles per day across thirty days gives one hundred and twenty articles. That is a worked arithmetic example, not a recommended quota. Some days will have fewer useful stories, and a careful report may require more than a day. The estimate should help you understand capacity without pressuring you to create filler.

Leave room for variation that you can reasonably anticipate. A local election meeting, sporting event or festival may increase the reporting workload. The purpose of spare capacity is to accommodate useful work when it arises. It is not an instruction to manufacture extra pages near the end of a billing period simply because the allowance has not been exhausted.

## Identify the actual constraint

Ask what prevented publication when a story was delayed. Was the account near its article limit, or was the reporter waiting for confirmation? Did image preparation take too long? Was there time to edit the draft? These are different constraints and need different responses. Buying a larger allowance does not automatically resolve a shortage of reporting time or an unclear editorial process.

Use a specific example from your own record. If three drafts waited for a final read, reserve editing time before increasing the daily target. If completed, checked stories could not be added because of the plan limit, investigate the available upgrade. A decision based on a visible bottleneck is easier to assess than a general feeling that the website needs something bigger.

## Check formats separately

News articles, explanatory blogs, video and ePaper editions may be governed by different plan features. Record which formats you currently use and which ones you genuinely intend to add. Do not assume an increased article allowance includes every other publishing tool. Ask how limits are counted and what happens when a format is unavailable in the selected subscription.

## Review quality alongside volume

Choose a small sample of published stories and read them as a visitor would. Look for a clear headline, useful context, readable paragraphs and a direct answer to the question the story raises. Check whether substantial corrections were necessary. This review does not require a numerical quality score. It requires a realistic understanding of whether the current workload leaves enough time to finish the work properly.

If the sample reveals rushed summaries or repeated omissions, increasing the target may make the problem worse. Consider a smaller set of well-prepared stories or a clearer checklist before publication. Conversely, a stable process with a consistent record of completed reporting may support a larger workload. The plan should serve that process rather than become the reason for setting an arbitrary target.

## Distinguish reader demand from page count

Record useful questions that readers ask through your actual contact channels. Several questions about one public service may justify a detailed explanatory article. That is different from publishing multiple near-identical pages around the same phrase. The first approach tries to solve a reader problem; the second can add navigation and maintenance work without adding meaningful information.

Do not invent audience data when you do not have it. If you have not measured traffic or enquiries, say that the evidence is limited and begin with the information available. A handful of reader questions can suggest an editorial experiment, but it does not establish a universal demand forecast. Keep examples clearly separated from claims about your publication's actual results.

## Make the upgrade question specific

Write a short request describing the current plan, recent usage and the additional requirement. For example, explain that checked article production has approached the allowance in two consecutive months and that an upcoming event may add more work. Ask which available option addresses that requirement and confirm the applicable price, billing duration and included features.

If you are considering a new format, ask for a practical demonstration of the relevant workflow. Confirm whether additional services or limits apply. Do not base the purchase on an unconfirmed future feature or a general marketing statement. Keep the response with your operating notes so the editorial team and the person paying for the subscription share the same expectations.

## Decide what to change next month

End the review with a small number of concrete decisions. You might retain the current plan, reserve more editing time and test one explanatory topic. Alternatively, you might upgrade because actual checked production consistently needs more room. In either case, record the reason and review it after another month instead of treating the decision as permanent.

A useful capacity review is short enough to repeat and detailed enough to explain what changed. It connects the website allowance with real reporting, realistic effort and identifiable reader needs. That gives a small publisher a better basis for spending and planning than choosing a plan solely because its largest number looks reassuring.
