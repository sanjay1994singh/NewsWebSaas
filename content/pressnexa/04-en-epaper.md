# How to organise a dependable daily ePaper workflow

A digital newspaper edition needs more than a completed file. Readers must be able to identify the correct date, open the right edition and read its pages comfortably. The publisher also needs a repeatable way to check the material before it becomes public. This guide explains a practical ePaper workflow for a small publication moving an existing print routine online. It focuses on preparation and editorial responsibility, rather than assuming software alone will organise the daily production process.

## Establish the edition structure

Start by deciding what counts as an edition. A city edition and a district edition may contain different pages even when they share a publication date. Give each a consistent name that a reader can recognise. Avoid internal production labels that only the designer understands. Keep the date format consistent across filenames, the edition title and any visible publication information. Small differences can make an archive confusing after several weeks of daily uploads.

Document the expected page order and who confirms the final version. If the front page is replaced late in production, everyone should know which file is authoritative. A simple internal register can record the edition date, version and final checker. This does not need to become a complicated administrative system. It needs to prevent the common mistake of publishing an earlier file because two versions were stored under almost identical names.

## Prepare pages for reading

Inspect the final pages at the size readers will actually use. Headlines may look clear on a design monitor while smaller text becomes difficult to read on a phone. Check representative pages containing columns, photographs and tables. Pay attention to missing characters and poorly reproduced captions. Use the actual publishing system for a sample test because the reader experience may differ from opening the original file on the designer's computer.

Choose a practical balance between readability and file size. A larger file is not automatically better if it contains unnecessarily heavy images, and an aggressively reduced file may lose important detail. Keep the original production files separately according to your own retention process. Before promising download or archive behaviour to subscribers, verify what the chosen service supports and test it with a real sample edition.

## Assign a publication checkpoint

Set an internal deadline for the production file and a separate checkpoint for publication. A checker should confirm the date, edition name, page count and page order. They should also open a few pages rather than relying only on filenames. The person approving the edition needs a clear way to report a problem and ask for a replacement. Without that checkpoint, a rushed upload can make yesterday's content appear to be today's newspaper.

For a small team, the same person may perform several roles. The important point is to separate the actions mentally and record completion. Prepare the file, inspect it, upload it and verify the public result. If an error appears, pause the next step until it is understood. A reliable routine is usually more valuable than an ambitious publication time that repeatedly leads to incorrect editions being released.

## Make the archive understandable

An archive should help a reader find a previous edition without knowing your internal filing system. Consistent dates and edition names are the foundation. Test a question such as finding last week's district edition. If the answer requires opening several indistinguishable items, the naming process needs improvement. Clear labels often solve a reader problem more effectively than adding another decorative section to the homepage.

Decide how the team handles a corrected edition. Record why a replacement was necessary and make significant editorial corrections understandable to readers where appropriate. Avoid silently using the same label for different material when that could cause confusion. This is a recommended editorial practice, not a claim that a particular automated correction tool is included in the plan. Confirm available controls with the provider.

## Connect the edition to useful reporting

An ePaper and a web article serve different reading needs. The edition preserves a newspaper layout, while an article can provide a direct route to a particular report. When a story is also published as a web article, keep the facts consistent and consider whether the reader needs additional context. Do not create an empty article whose only purpose is to make someone click through several pages to reach the information.

Use material that the publication has the right to publish. A digital edition should not be treated as permission to copy another newspaper's pages or photographs. Maintain a practical record of commissioned images, supplied material and relevant permissions. This helps the team answer questions later and keeps the production process focused on its own reporting. Original commentary and accurate attribution matter in both print-style editions and ordinary website articles.

## Plan for delays and handover

Identify what happens when the regular uploader is unavailable or the final pages arrive late. Give the backup person clear instructions and suitable account access through the service's supported arrangements. Avoid relying on a single unlabelled folder on one personal device. Keep contact details for production and technical support in an internal location that the responsible team can reach.

At the end of the first week, review actual problems: wrong labels, difficult pages, repeated replacements or unclear responsibilities. Improve the routine before adding more editions.
