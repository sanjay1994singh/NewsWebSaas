# Build an update routine for explanatory articles in a small newsroom

An explanatory article can remain useful after the event that inspired it has passed. That makes it different from a short report tied to a particular meeting or announcement. It also creates a maintenance responsibility. A guide that once explained a process accurately may become confusing when a contact detail, requirement or procedure changes. For an independent publisher, a manageable update routine is more useful than publishing a large collection that nobody has time to maintain.

## Start with a narrow reader question

Choose one question that the article will answer. A guide might explain how to prepare for a local public meeting or how to find the original notice behind a community announcement. Keep the scope small enough that you can verify the necessary information. An article trying to explain every public service in a district will be harder to write accurately and harder to keep current.

Write the question at the top of your working notes. Then list the information a reader needs to reach an answer. Remove material that does not help with that task. This is not an instruction to make every guide brief. It is a way to ensure that a longer article contains relevant explanation rather than unrelated paragraphs added to reach a target length.

## Identify details that may change

Some parts of a guide explain general background, while others depend on current information. Mark the changeable details in your internal notes. Office contacts, submission arrangements, opening times and charges are examples of information that may require a later check. Do not assume that a detail remains correct because several other websites repeat it.

Use an appropriate primary source when the article depends on a current process. Keep the source address and the date you checked it. If two sources disagree, resolve the disagreement before presenting one version as settled. Where a necessary fact cannot be established, narrow the claim or hold the article until you can confirm it. A confident sentence is not a substitute for verification.

## Create a small maintenance record

You do not need a complex application to begin. An internal record can contain the article address, its main question, the responsible editor and the details most likely to change. Add the source used for verification and the last review date. Avoid storing unnecessary personal information in this record. Its purpose is to help the editor find and assess the article later.

For a solo reporter, responsibility may always point to the same person. The record still matters because memory is unreliable after several months of reporting. It also helps a temporary replacement understand why a guide needs attention. A short note explaining that a contact detail requires confirmation is more actionable than a general instruction to check everything.

## Review when there is a reason

A fixed review interval can be useful, but it should not be the only trigger. A new announcement, a reader correction or a changed source page may justify an earlier review. Record these signals when they arise during normal reporting. If a fresh news story reveals that an older guide is affected, include the guide in the follow-up work.

Do not change the publication date merely to make an unchanged article appear new. Review the actual content and record meaningful corrections or additions in an appropriate way. The reader should be able to understand the current information. A maintenance routine should improve accuracy, not create the impression of constant freshness without substantive editorial work.

## Separate updates from corrections

An update adds information that became available later. A correction addresses something that was wrong or misleading in the earlier article. The distinction helps the editor decide what explanation the reader needs. A new meeting date may be an update, while replacing a date that was incorrectly reported may require a correction.

Use plain wording when a significant change needs explanation. Avoid an internal note that makes sense only to the writer. At the same time, do not expose unnecessary private details while describing a correction. The practical question is what a reader needs to understand the reliable version of the information and any important difference from what appeared before.

## Make the article useful on its own

A reader may arrive directly from a shared link without having seen the original news report. Include enough context to make the guide understandable independently. Explain the purpose early, use descriptive headings and give practical examples where they clarify a step. Link to the original source when it helps the reader verify or continue the task.

Label hypothetical examples as examples. Do not invent a resident, a successful application or a measured outcome and present it as a real case. An example can show how to organise information without claiming that a particular event occurred. That distinction allows the article to be clear and useful while remaining honest about the evidence behind it.

## Match the collection to your capacity

The number of guides you can maintain matters as much as the number you can publish. Begin with subjects closely connected to your regular reporting. Familiarity with the area may help you notice when information changes, though it does not remove the need for verification. Avoid opening many unrelated topic areas simply because the website has room for them.

## Check whether the guide still earns its place

During review, ask whether the article answers a distinct reader question. If two guides now cover almost the same ground, consider how their roles can be clarified. Do not maintain duplication solely to preserve the size of the collection. If information is no longer dependable, decide what revision or other editorial action is appropriate rather than leaving it untouched.

End each review with a recorded outcome: checked with no substantive change, updated, corrected or awaiting information. This makes the next review easier and avoids repeatedly investigating the same uncertainty. A small newsroom can then treat explanatory publishing as a continuing service to readers. The value comes from clear answers that remain dependable, not from the number of pages created in a single campaign.
